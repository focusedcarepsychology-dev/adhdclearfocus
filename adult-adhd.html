Adult ADHD: look for the pattern, not a stereotype

ADHD in adults can affect attention, organisation, time management, emotional regulation and impulse control. The key clinical question is not whether a symptom exists, but whether a persistent developmental pattern causes meaningful impairment.

Short answer: Adult ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition. Symptoms may change in appearance with age, but clinically relevant ADHD reflects a persistent pattern that began in development and affects functioning across important areas of life.

Common adult patterns

Attention

Losing track in meetings, reading without retaining, overlooking detail, or struggling to sustain effort on low-reward tasks.

Executive function

Difficulty sequencing tasks, prioritising, starting, switching, remembering intentions and completing multi-step work.

Time and urgency

Underestimating duration, relying on last-minute pressure, or becoming absorbed in one activity at the expense of others.

Impact

The same underlying difficulties may show up in work, education, finances, relationships, driving, sleep routines or household management.

Why childhood history matters

ADHD is developmental. A proper assessment therefore looks for evidence that relevant difficulties were present earlier in life, even if they were not recognised as ADHD at the time.

What can look similar

Sleep problems, anxiety, depression, trauma, autism, substance use, physical illness and severe stress can all affect concentration or executive function. These possibilities may coexist with ADHD and need careful consideration.

Sources
